Nyaya Mitra — an AI lawyer that cannot invent law

Problem 2 · Kuku AI Generalist Assignment · Security deposits, unpaid salary, consumer refunds · English / Hindi / Hinglish

Why this problem

Problem 1 is a ranking task with a human already in the loop: an editor sees every promo before it ships, so a bad suggestion costs a few minutes. Problem 2 has no such backstop. Someone with no money and no lawyer will act on what the tool says — send a notice, miss a deadline, walk into the wrong forum — and a confident wrong answer does real harm. That asymmetry is why I picked it, and the brief points straight at the hard part: “AI models confidently invent laws, sections and past cases, so tell us how yours avoids that.” I treated that sentence as the whole specification.

Assumptions where the brief was silent

ScopeThree problems in depth, not many in breadth. Depth is what lets the tool name a forum and a deadline instead of giving general encouragement.
LanguageEnglish, Devanagari Hindi and Romanised Hinglish — Hinglish because that is how most people actually type. Not other languages: I would be generating legal phrasing I cannot verify, which contradicts the point of the build.
The userHas a phone, not a lawyer, and wants to know what to do on Monday. So the output is a deadline, a place to go, a fee and a next step.
SuccessOne correct concrete step the person would not otherwise have taken — usually a written demand or a notice, not litigation.
No case lawA deliberate capability gap, explained below.

How it works

The system is split by what must be right.

  story ──► [ extract ] ◄── model      ┌─ kb/provisions.json ────────┐
                 │                     │ 30 hand-written entries,    │
          structured facts             │ source-linked. The ONLY     │
                 ▼                     │ law that may be cited.      │
   ┌─────────────────────────┐         └─────────────┬───────────────┘
   │ casefile.py — NO MODEL  │                       │ only these ids
   │ deadline · forum · fee  │       model prose ──► │
   │ escalation to a human   │                 [ guard.py ]
   └───────────┬─────────────┘                       │ stripped + reported
               └─────────────────────────────►  what the person sees

Computed, never generated. The limitation deadline, days remaining, the forum, the filing-fee slab and the decision to escalate to a real lawyer are arithmetic over the dates the person gave. A model is confidently wrong about exactly these — it invents a pecuniary limit, it miscounts twelve months — so it never touches them. Every number on screen can be recomputed by hand.

The model does three narrow jobs: read a messy story into a form (it is genuinely better than regex at “maine 10 August ko ghar khaali kiya”), say the situation back in the person’s language, and write one factual paragraph of a notice. It is given no legal authority in any of them.

Everything it writes passes a guard:

  • Citations are ids. The model may only emit [[CPA2019_S69]]; the id must exist in the KB and have been supplied for that turn; the visible citation text is rendered by us from the KB. The words of the law never come from the model.
  • Loose references are caught — and so is the sentence around them. Removing only the citation from “under Section 23 of the Tenancy Rules he owes a penalty” leaves the invented penalty standing, which is the more believable half.
  • Case law is always refused. The KB holds no judgments, so there is nothing to check one against. Every case-shaped string is treated as fabricated whether or not it happens to be real.

The adversarial corpus is the real evidence. It holds the failures a general model actually produces: an invented section for a rule with no statutory basis, a fabricated Supreme Court judgment, the Negotiable Instruments Act applied to a tenancy dispute, IPC sections for a civil debt, UK housing law, Article 21 for a money claim — plus two correct answers that must survive untouched, because a filter that mangles good output gets switched off within a week.

The model produced:

“In Sharma v. Gupta (2018) 7 SCC 412, the Supreme Court held that a landlord withholding a security deposit without itemised proof commits an unfair trade practice. You can rely on this directly.”

The user saw:

“[A claim about case law was removed here: this assistant has no verified database of judgments and will not cite them.] You can rely on this directly.”

Where it fails

  • State law is the hole. Tenancy and shops-and-establishments law is state-specific and the KB has none of it. The system names the route and refuses to state a section or a limit. Correct, but unsatisfying: a user in Maharashtra gets “check your state’s Rent Act”, not an answer.
  • No case law at all, by design. A real lawyer cites precedent; this cannot, and says so.
  • Volatile numbers go stale. Pecuniary limits, fees and the wage ceiling are flagged volatility: high and dated. They need a human to re-check, not a model.
  • Extraction is the weakest component. Two bugs worth naming, both caught by test cases rather than by reading code: Hindi and Hinglish are verb-final, so every date arrived before the verb and my English-ordered patterns dropped all of them — and no date means no deadline, the single most important output. Separately, Python’s \b does not work around Devanagari (matras are non-spacing marks, not word characters), so every Hindi keyword silently scored zero.
  • The guard is regex over English-shaped legal references. A model writing invented law in fluent Hindi prose would evade it. First thing I would fix.

How it scales to every problem, and what it costs

Cost is near zero and flat: ~900 input / ~350 output tokens per grounded answer, so a full consultation is under 10k tokens — inside the Gemini free tier, a fraction of a paisa on paid Flash. The expensive part is not inference. It is the knowledge base, and that is a human writing thirty entries once per domain.

That is the honest scaling story: adding a problem is a day of careful writing, not a prompt change. A legal-aid NGO could own provisions.json without touching code, and load-time validation means a mistake in it stops the program rather than reaching a user. The whole pipeline is standard library only, so it runs offline on a cheap machine with no model at all — losing the conversational polish and keeping every legal guarantee. Swapping to a stronger model is a flag (--model gemini:…); no vendor SDK is imported outside its own adapter file.

Humans stay in the loop in two places that matter: rule-based escalation hands off to a real lawyer on safety, criminal matters, expired limitation and anything out of scope; and every notice is marked a draft for the user to check before sending.

AI tools, and where they got it wrong

Built with Claude (Claude Code) throughout — architecture, knowledge-base drafting, code. I used it hard and checked it harder.

Where it got it wrong. Asked to draft the knowledge base, it produced confident, fluent, wrongly-numbered provisions — a “Section 17 of the Model Tenancy Act” for deposit refund timelines, and a Supreme Court judgment that does not exist. That is not a footnote to this project; it is the project. I kept those outputs, put them in testcases/hallucinations.json, and built the guard to catch exactly them. The tool that wrote the system also generated its adversarial test set, by failing.

A second, smaller one: it initially wrote the Hindi interface strings in Romanised Hindi for Devanagari users. Fluent, friendly, and the small discourtesy that tells someone a tool was not built for them.

Run it: python run.py — no API key, no network, no account. Evidence: outputs/guard_report.md (before/after for all 15 adversarial items), outputs/report.md, outputs/*.md (every consultation). Tests: python check_guard.py, python tests/test_nyaya.py.
